We Rarely Fall in Love With People as They Truly Are

by Don Madollar

We rarely fall in love with people as they truly are. More often, we fall in love with the versions of them we have quietly constructed in our minds. We gather their words, gestures, silences, and occasional kindness, then arrange these fragments into an image that satisfies something within us. We call it love, but sometimes it is simply our imagination finding a home in another person.

Perhaps this is why love so often begins with wonder and ends with disappointment. In the beginning, we do not necessarily see the person; we see the possibility of who they might be. We dress their imperfections in the finest garments of our expectations, interpret their silences as depth, their distance as mystery, and their smallest gestures as evidence of something extraordinary. We do not fall for the person alone; we fall for the meaning we have assigned to them.

But time is an unforgiving tailor. Eventually, the fabric begins to wear, the imagined qualities lose their colour, and the person beneath the illusion starts to emerge. What once appeared magnificent becomes ordinary, not necessarily because they have changed, but because our imagination can no longer conceal what reality has always been. Disappointment, then, is sometimes nothing more than the distance between who someone is and who we needed them to be.

Yet perhaps the tragedy is not that we idealise people, but that we expect them to remain inside the identities we have created for them. We want them to keep fitting the version we first admired, forgetting that human beings are not finished portraits. They change, contradict themselves, grow unfamiliar, and occasionally become strangers even to themselves. Loving someone, therefore, may require the difficult discipline of abandoning our imagined version of them and learning to encounter the actual person, repeatedly.

There is another complication: two people can share the same words and still inhabit entirely different meanings. They can say I love you, believe it sincerely, and yet be speaking from different emotional worlds. One may mean security, another desire; one may mean permanence, another companionship. Each carries a private universe of memories, fears, expectations, and interpretations into the relationship. What appears to be a meeting of souls may sometimes be two separate imaginations briefly agreeing on the same language.

Perhaps, in the end, we are not as acquainted with one another as we pretend to be. We know the portions people reveal, the stories we construct around them, and the feelings their presence awakens within us. The rest remains inaccessible, hidden behind the walls of individual consciousness. Even intimacy cannot entirely abolish the distance between two minds.

And perhaps this is what makes understanding so exhausting: the endless attempt to translate another person's inner world using the limited vocabulary of our own. We spend our lives interpreting gestures, questioning intentions, revising conclusions, and searching for certainty in things that were never entirely certain. Sometimes, the mind becomes so occupied with explaining existence that it forgets to experience it.

Perhaps the greatest difficulty in loving another human being is learning to see them without the interference of our own imagination.
