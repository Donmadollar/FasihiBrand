The Umbrella on Moi Avenue

by FasihiBrand Editorial

The rain began at four, the way it always did in April, without warning and without mercy. Amani stood under the narrow shelter of a closed pharmacy on Moi Avenue, clutching a folder of job applications that was growing softer by the minute.

“You’ll need more than that roof,” said a voice beside her. A man in a grey coat held out a black umbrella, already tilted toward her side. “I was going to wait out the rain, but you look like someone with somewhere to be.”

“Interview at five,” Amani said. “My fourth this month.”

“Then this is going to be the one.” He said it so simply, without a trace of pity, that she laughed for the first time all week.

His name was Baraka. He walked her four blocks, talking about nothing: the price of mandazi, the matatu driver who played gospel at full volume. He kept the umbrella over her the whole way while the rain soaked his own shoulder.

At the glass doors of the office, she turned to return it. “Keep it,” he said. “Bring it back when you have good news.”

“I don’t even know where to find you.”

He smiled. “Every Friday I buy my coffee at the stall by the old bus stop. The one with the blue kettle.”

She got the job. On Friday she went to the stall with the umbrella and a nervous heart. He was there, as if the whole week had been holding its breath.

“Good news?” he asked.

“Good news,” she said.

They drank their coffee standing, because the stall had no chairs, and the next Friday they did the same. By the fifth Friday, Baraka brought a second cup of tea in case she preferred it. By the tenth, she had learned that he fixed clocks for a living, and that he kept every clock in his shop running five minutes early, so nobody he loved would ever be late.

In December, the rain returned. This time Amani was the one waiting at the stall, holding the black umbrella high. When Baraka arrived, soaked and laughing, she stepped close and tilted it over them both.

“I kept it for you,” she said. “I think I’d like to keep you too.”

Baraka looked at her, then at the rain, then at the whole city running a little late, and for once he did not mind.

“Then stay,” he said. “There is always room under here for two.”
