What My Younger Self Taught Me Without Meaning To

by FasihiBrand Editorial

Every so often I come across something from an earlier version of my life: an old message, a photograph, a page of handwriting that looks like it belongs to someone I once knew well. I read it the way you read a letter from an old friend, half fond and half wincing. That person was so certain about so many things. Their confidence was loud; their understanding was still arriving.

I used to be hard on that younger self. I saw only the mistakes: the words said too quickly, the trust given too freely, the lessons that cost more than they should have. I would have liked to lean across time and offer a few warnings. But the longer I think about it, the less sure I am that I would be who I am without those detours.

When I was younger, I believed adulthood was a place you arrived at, like a station on a map. You would step off the train with the answers in your pocket, and the world would finally treat you with respect. Nobody told me it would feel less like arriving and more like learning to walk steadily on ground that keeps shifting.

What I know now is smaller and more useful than what I expected to know. That most people are doing their best with what they have been given. That apologising is a strength. That rest is not laziness, and that being kind to yourself is not the same as excusing yourself. That some of the most important chapters of a life look, at the time, like nothing more than a difficult week.

I also understand now that growing up was never meant to be tidy. It happens in the gaps between plans, in the conversations that change your mind, in the failures that quietly redirect you. Nobody becomes themselves in a straight line.

So these days I try to speak to my younger self with more gentleness. Not to correct them, but to thank them. They took the first awkward steps so that I could walk more calmly. They were brave in ways they did not know. And they kept going, long before they could see why.

Perhaps that is the real measure of growing up: not how few mistakes you made, but how kindly you are able to remember the person who made them.
